In a discipline that tends toward defining itself (most often begrudgingly) in the negative - an architecture's context, a figure's ground, a city's reprieve - D. W. Winnicott's Playing and Reality offers a refreshing critique of binary assumptions.
Here Winnicott argues that the development in a child of the capacity to play is the root of creative life, engagement with and contribution to culture: that it is the prerequisite for really living.

This capacity to play, Winnicott contends, emerges in early childhood in the space between a child and their outer environment at the very moment that the child begins to recognise that the world is not, in fact, an extension of themselves; that there are indeed other things out there.
This is a precarious moment, and to be navigated effectively it requires a degree of confidence on the part of the child that these emerging others (particularly, Winnicott argues, the mother) will remain reliably constant even as the child begins the destructive work of extricating a self from a context. Indeed it is only in this recognition of the other, Winnicott contends, that a self can really start to be defined. 
In this simplest of senses, and as everyone seems to be reminding us these days, our lives are contingent.

But it is this space that Winnicott identifies between self and other that is interesting in the context of this research. Winnicott defines play as 

'*always on the theoretical line between the subjective and that which is objectively perceived.'* 59
and that
*'In playing, the child manipulates external phenomena in the service of the dream and invests chosen external phenomena with dream meaning and feeling.'* 60

The space of play is a space in which a child can still invest external phenomena with some of the self-same omnipotence that characterised the child's earliest conceptions. The world, however, is newly recognised as bringing logics, constraints, and rules of its own to the table. Playing, Winnicott argues, happens in this place of interface and slippage between inside and out and is a prerequisite to the later skills of concentration, cultural engagement and living rather than surviving life.

Winnicott's definition of play offers a helpful challenge to the 'all external order is tyranny' position posited by some landscape architects in playground design and evident more loosely even in the antagonism with architecture raised at the opening of this piece.
In 1971 landscape architect [[Simon Nicholson]] originated the influential Theory of Loose Parts in which he argued that 

*'in any environment, both the degree of inventiveness and creativity, and the possibility of discovery, are directly proportional to the number and kind of variables in it'*
and that
*'adults in the form of ... landscape architects ... have had all the fun playing with their own materials, concepts and planning-alternatives, and the builders have had all the fun building the environments out of real materials; and thus has all the fun and creativity been stolen'*

In opposition to this position, Winnicott might argue that coming into contact with something else, something irreducibly other, something more than putty for the expression of our internal selves, is in fact the very point and function of play.
